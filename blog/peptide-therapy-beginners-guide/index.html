    Peptide Therapy Explained: BPC-157, CJC-1295, NAD+ and What the Evidence Says

    Oakwell MD care team · August 31, 2026 · 6 min read

    Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. Insulin is a peptide, and so is the GLP-1 hormone your gut releases after meals. In wellness clinics, the word usually refers to a smaller group of injectable or nasal compounds marketed for recovery or aging. Evidence for them varies widely. Some are FDA-approved for narrow uses, and many are not approved for anything.

    Approval status matters for a practical reason. Approval means controlled trials showed the drug works for a defined use and the factory meets federal standards. A compounded peptide skips that process. That does not make it useless, though it leaves you with less proof and fewer quality checks than an approved product carries.

    BPC-157

    BPC-157 is a lab-made fragment of a protein found in stomach juice. Rodent research hints at faster tendon and gut healing, yet controlled human trials are close to nonexistent. It is not FDA-approved. The agency has flagged it as a compounding substance with potential safety risks, mainly because human data is so thin. Rules here have shifted in recent years, so ask your provider about its current status.

    People usually take BPC-157 as a daily injection under the skin near the injured area, or as an oral capsule. Clinic reports mention injection-site redness and lightheadedness, though no one has tracked side effects in a formal study. There is also a theoretical concern. Animal data show it encourages new blood vessel growth, which raises questions for anyone with a cancer history.

    CJC-1295, ipamorelin and sermorelin

    These compounds prompt the pituitary gland to release more of your own growth hormone. Sermorelin held an FDA approval for pediatric growth hormone deficiency until the manufacturer withdrew it, and today it is compounded. CJC-1295 and ipamorelin have never been FDA-approved. Claims about fat loss and better sleep come mostly from small or short studies. Side effects can include water retention and higher blood sugar, and flushing at the injection site is common.

    Growth hormone releasers can affect blood sugar and are usually avoided in people with active cancer. A provider will typically check IGF-1, a blood marker of growth hormone activity, before starting and again after a few months. Injections are often taken at bedtime. Most protocols run in cycles.

    Tesamorelin is the exception. Sold as Egrifta, it carries FDA approval for reducing abdominal fat in people with HIV-related lipodystrophy. Using it for general weight loss or aging in people without that condition is off-label, and insurance almost never covers that use. A provider should check blood sugar before and during treatment.

    NAD+

    NAD+ is a coenzyme every cell uses to turn food into energy, and levels drop with age. It is sold as IV infusions, shots or nasal sprays, mostly marketed for energy and healthy aging. Human data is limited. Oral precursors such as nicotinamide riboside raise blood NAD+ levels in studies, though clear real-world benefits have not been proven. Fast IV infusions commonly cause flushing and chest tightness, so they are usually run slowly.

    NAD+ injections are usually given a few times a week at first, then less often. They are not FDA-approved for any condition, and most of the excitement comes from animal research on aging and cell energy rather than from human trials. Expect a mild flush.

    Questions to ask before starting a peptide

    • Is this product FDA-approved, and for what?
    • What human studies support the use I am interested in?
    • Which licensed pharmacy compounds it, and is it a 503A or 503B facility?
    • What side effects should I watch for, and what labs will you check?
    • How long will I take it, and how will we know if it works?

    Skip anything sold online as research use only. Those products are not made for human use, and purity testing is often missing. Bremelanotide, sold as Vyleesi, is an FDA-approved peptide for low sexual desire in premenopausal women, a reminder that a peptide can be approved once proper trials are completed.
